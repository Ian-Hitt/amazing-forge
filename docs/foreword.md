# Foreword

*Why I made this game, and what I hope it does.*

My name is Ian Hitt, and I've been playing tabletop roleplaying games for about ten years. This book is the game I kept wishing existed — stitched together from three games I love, plus a few stubborn opinions about what a story at the table should feel like.

## Three games I was chasing

The first is **Amazing Tales**, which I've played with my own little kids. It's astonishingly simple. The children pick four things they're good at, build a quick little world, and then you just *play* — they drive the story, and the grown-up mostly gets to be a guide along for the ride. My first instinct was that so few rules would leave us grasping for ideas. I was completely wrong. Kids have endless imagination; the rules were never the thing holding a story up. That lesson stuck with me.

The second is **Ironsworn: Starforged**. I fell for its mechanics harder than almost any game I've played. What it understood is that *behind every dice-rolling game is an author doing math* — quietly working out how many rolls it should take, on average, to get something done. Most games bury that math under hit points and damage and action economy, under healing rules and "adventuring days," when really the only question any of us ever wanted answered was: **roughly how many rolls should this take?** Starforged just says it out loud. You draw a row of boxes, you fill them when you succeed, and you're done when it's mostly full. To me that was everything other games were doing, in its simplest honest form, missing almost nothing — and far faster for telling a story. My only problem was that Starforged is built specifically for space-opera sci-fi, and I wanted to tell every other kind of story too.[^roll]

The third is **Fate** — a game that is deliberately genre- and setting-agnostic. Not a story so much as a toolbox: a set of rules you point at *whatever* story you feel like telling. That openness was the last piece.

So that's the target I was aiming at: the **approachability of Amazing Tales**, the **simplicity of Starforged**, and the **openness of Fate**.

## Make a movie, not a video game

The rest came from a feeling I couldn't shake. So many tabletop games end up playing like a co-op choose-your-own-adventure *video game* you narrate out loud. That tends to wreck the pacing — a fight in some games can eat an hour of real time to cover twelve seconds of story — and it quietly pulls attention away from the one thing I actually come to the table for: the story.

I didn't want this game to feel like a video game at all. I wanted it to feel like **a room full of TV writers and actors building the script in real time and then jumping into character to play it out.** The whole game is pointed at the movie you're making together. The dice are there to turn the drama up at the right moments, not to simulate a physics engine.

And once you decide you're writing a story, there's no reason to ignore the tools people already use to write good ones. The hero's journey. The "save the cat" beat sheet. Three-, four-, and five-act structure. Character archetypes. Theme. These are superb instruments for building stories on the page and the screen — so why shouldn't a tabletop game reach for them too? This one does.

## What I hope it does

I hope this game gets out of your way. I hope a ten-year-old and a table of adults can sit down with it and, within minutes, be telling a story none of them could have written alone — fast, cinematic, and entirely their own. If it does that, it did its job.

Now go make something amazing.

— Ian Hitt

[^roll]:
    One honest confession for the rules-watchers: the actual dice roll in this book — roll 2d6, add a little, read the result on a tiered scale — comes from the *Apocalypse World* lineage, not from Starforged's own resolution mechanic. Sorry, Starforged. I borrowed your boxes and somebody else's dice.
