# Playing with Kids

*How to tune the game for a younger table.*

*Lights, Camera, Action!* isn't a kids' game — it's written for anyone who wants to tell fast, cinematic stories. But it's built simply enough that kids can genuinely play it, and with a handful of small adjustments it runs beautifully at a table full of them. None of what follows is a new rule to learn: some of it is rules you can safely set aside — the game is modular enough that pieces lift out clean — and the rest is table technique.

Start with **Teaching the Game** (the previous chapter): teach four things, start playing, teach everything else the moment it comes up. That advice goes double here. Kids learn every game by playing it, and no kid ever sat still for a rules speech.

---

## Tips for a younger table

**Consider skipping Challenges.** A Challenge is a pacing tool, not a resolution tool — it stretches one big moment across several rolls so a set-piece takes up more table time. Many kids don't want that; they cut from scene to scene like a movie trailer: cross the ravine, trick the guard, grab the treasure, *next.* If that's your table, drop the subsystem: one roll per scene, narrate the result big, move on (a huge climactic moment can still be two or three rolls — just no track). Nothing else depends on Challenges — Milestones still mark story headway, Readiness still drops on a Weak Hit or a Miss, and the Devil's Bargain, Doubles, and the Antagonist Track all key off ordinary rolls.

**Play Episodes, not Movies.** Three Milestones, a five-box Antagonist Track, a whole story in one sitting. Kids' tables move fast, so an Episode may take closer to an hour than three. Finish the story while everyone still wants more — or stop on a cliffhanger, which kids love even harder than adults do.

**Set the dial to playful.** Every genre runs on a playful↔serious dial (Chapter 18). For kids, pin it at playful: setbacks are funny or exciting, never scary, and the antagonist schemes like a cartoon — menacing enough to root against, never enough to follow anyone to bed. When the bad guys close in, show them *almost* winning, gloating, getting away with the map.

**Say yes to Assets.** Whether an Asset applies is already meant to be argued from the story — with kids, be generous. If they can explain why their *Dragon-Taming Kit* helps here, it counts. That's not going soft; that's the exact storytelling muscle the game is trying to train. (Keep the Stats honest, though — "a chase is Quick" protects the quieter kids from losing arguments to the louder ones.)

**Hand them the narration.** The rule is that a hit means *you* narrate the change — and kids are the best in the world at this. Ask "so what does that look like?" after every roll and get out of the way.

**Lean on the safety net.** Heroes can't die — Readiness is a "time to fall back" signal, not a doom meter — and a Recovery Scene heals everyone completely, for free, as often as the story allows. Use it constantly. And when Readiness drops, narrate scrapes, lost gear, and slapstick tumbles, never injuries.

**Keep the Devil's Bargain.** If you're trimming rules, don't trim this one — *"you can have the win… but the bad guys get one step closer. Deal?"* is a real decision a six-year-old can weigh, argue about, and remember all week.

**Skip Growth for one-shots.** Growth, Boons, the Growth Track — that bookkeeping pays off across a Season. For a single afternoon Episode, leave it in the book.

**Pass the spotlight.** Go around the table so every hero gets a scene. The loudest kid will happily roll every roll if you let them; a simple "and while that's happening — what's *your* hero doing?" keeps everyone in the movie.

**Anyone can soften a scene.** Agree up front: if anything in the story feels too scary, anyone can say so and it changes — no explanation needed, no fuss. Kids use this less than you'd expect, but knowing it's there is what lets them relax into the story.

---

## Who runs it?

Kids around ten and up can run the game entirely themselves, Co-op style — it was built light enough for exactly that. For younger kids, the smoothest setup is an adult or a teen as the Guide: someone to hold the tracks, voice the antagonist, and keep the camera moving. Either way, run it straight out of **Teaching the Game** — four things, then action.
