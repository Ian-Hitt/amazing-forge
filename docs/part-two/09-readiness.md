## Chapter 9 — Readiness (Complete)

Readiness is your hero's single resource — body, mind, and supplies rolled into one 0–9 track (Chapter 2). This chapter covers the whole life of that number: how it goes **down** (Paying the Price) and how it comes back **up** (Recovery). They're two sides of one loop, so they live together here.

A quick orientation before the details:

- Readiness only ever **falls during action**, on a Weak Hit (−1) or a Miss (−2).
- It only ever **rises during safe lulls**, through a Recovery Scene (+3) or finishing a Quest (+6).
- It's capped at **9** and never refills automatically — every point matters.

---

## Part A — Pay the Price

Paying the Price is what happens on a **Weak Hit** or a **Miss** — the complication that follows when a roll doesn't go cleanly. Mechanically it's simple: a Weak Hit costs **1 Readiness**, a Miss costs **2.** But the number is the *smallest* part of it. Paying the Price is where setbacks become story, and handling it well is most of what makes Amazing Forge feel like a movie instead of a spreadsheet.

### Choosing the Price

The price is **the most obvious negative thing that would happen right now, in this Scene.** That's the whole guideline. Before you mark off any Readiness, answer the question the fiction is already asking: *given what just happened, what's the most logical — or most dramatic — way this goes wrong?* Then describe that, and let the Readiness loss be the mechanical echo of it.

Two principles keep this on track:

- **Fiction first, number second.** Don't say "you lose 1 Readiness" and stop. Say "the rope snaps and you slam back down onto the ledge, knocking the wind out of you — that's 1 Readiness." The loss should always be attached to something that *happened*.
- **Make it a setback, not a fee.** A good price feels like a complication in a film — a door slamming, an alarm tripping, a friend cut off from the group. A bad price feels like a parking ticket. If the price doesn't change the Scene in some way, reach for a more interesting one.

Remember what Readiness represents (Chapter 2): body, mind, *and* supplies. That gives you three flavors of price to draw from. The cost of a Miss can be a physical knock, a hit to morale or nerve, *or* lost and broken gear — whichever the moment makes most sense of. A ghost's shriek that rattles a hero drains Readiness exactly like a fall does.

### The d10 Table

You should usually just *pick* the most obvious price — it's faster and almost always better than rolling. But when the table draws a blank, or you want a jolt of surprise, roll **1d10** on the table below and bend the result to fit the Scene.

| d10 | The Price | How It Looks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Environment Rebels** | The floor crumbles, a pipe bursts with hot steam, or a door slams shut, sealing your exit. |
| 2 | **You Lose Gear or Power** | Your flashlight dies, your rope snaps, or your shield's battery drains to critical. |
| 3 | **You Get Separated** | A falling boulder blocks the path between you and your friends, or you're cornered alone. |
| 4 | **An Enemy Gains Ground** | A foe reaches the high ground, blocks your escape, or gets into prime position to strike. |
| 5 | **A New Obstacle Appears** | An alarm blares, reinforcements arrive, or a sudden magical storm rolls in. |
| 6 | **Your Action Backfires** | Your spell lights the wrong curtains, you make a crash that alerts foes, or you trip a hidden trap. |
| 7 | **A Costly Decision** | To save a dropped item (or a friend), you must take **1 extra Readiness loss.** |
| 8 | **You Are Rattled & Shaken** | A ghost's screech chills your bones, or a wave of self-doubt saps your focus and energy. |
| 9 | **A Hidden Truth Is Revealed** | The map you've followed is outdated, or the lava monster is immune to normal weapons. |
| 10 | **Roll Twice or Amplify** | Two complications hit at once, or one threat escalates into a dramatic movie moment. |

A couple of notes on using it. Entry **7** is the only one that changes the number — it offers a *choice*: accept an extra point of loss to hold onto something you'd otherwise drop. Entry **10** is your "turn it up" result — use it when the Scene can take a real escalation. And nothing stops you from reading a rolled result loosely; the table is a spark, not a verdict.

### Guide Guidance: Making It Hurt Right

(This is written for a Guide, but in Co-op play it's just as useful — the player who rolled has narrative rights over their own price, and the table can lean on all of it.)

The art of a good price is making it **push the story forward** rather than simply taxing the hero. A few habits help:

- **Aim the price at the plot, not just the hero.** "You take 2 Readiness" is bookkeeping. "The bridge you crossed collapses behind you — now the only way out is *forward*, deeper in" is a price that *moves the movie.* The best complications close off easy options and open dramatic ones.
- **Escalate what's already tense.** Look at what the Scene is about and turn that dial. In a chase, the price tightens the gap. In a stealth Scene, it edges the alarm closer. In a social Scene, it turns the room against you. The price should feel inevitable in hindsight.
- **Vary the three flavors.** If every price has been a physical knock, make the next one a hit to morale or a lost piece of gear. Rotating between body, mind, and supplies keeps Readiness loss from feeling repetitive and keeps all three meanings of the track alive.
- **Match the size of the moment.** A Miss in a tense climax can warrant the dramatic, table-flipping version (entry 10 territory). A Weak Hit on a small Scene wants a light, quick complication. Don't spend a huge consequence on a minor beat, or vice versa.
- **Never make it feel like a punishment for trying.** The price answers *what goes wrong*, not *you shouldn't have done that*. Heroes are supposed to attempt bold things and sometimes pay for them — that's the genre. Keep the tone "ooh, that's bad!" excitement, not "gotcha."

Used this way, Paying the Price becomes the engine of complications that the whole adventure runs on: every Weak Hit and Miss is a little gift of new trouble to play with.

> **A clarifying note on the Antagonist Track:** Paying the Price and the **Antagonist Track** (Chapter 10) are completely separate. A Weak Hit or Miss costs Readiness and nothing else — it never advances the villain. The bad guys gain ground only when the heroes **flee** a Challenge (Chapter 8). Don't conflate the two: the price is what a roll costs *you*; the villain clock moves only when you choose to retreat.

## Part B — Recovery

Readiness only falls in the heat of the action, and you never get it back mid-Challenge. But whenever the story reaches a **genuinely safe lull** — the danger has passed, even just for now — the heroes can play a **Recovery Scene** to get back on their feet.

### What Triggers a Recovery Scene

A Recovery Scene is **gated by the fiction, not by the mechanics.** It isn't a button you press between Challenges; it's a quiet beat the story has actually earned — the chase is over, the camp is made, the ship is limping but safe for the night. If real danger is still present, there's no Recovery Scene to be had.

It's also a *slower* story beat, not just a number change: bandaging wounds by a campfire, repairing the ship, visiting a healer, a heart-to-heart that steadies everyone's nerves. Play it out a little — this is where the drama and the characters' feelings get room to breathe, which is half its value. **Reaching low Readiness is the table's cue** that it's time to look for a safe moment and take one.

### The Heal: +3, Partial, Capped

In a Recovery Scene, **each hero recovers +3 Readiness**, never above the cap of **9.** You **never roll** for it and you **never lose anything** by taking one — it is meant to feel safe, not risky.

The heal is **deliberately partial.** +3 gets a battered hero back into fighting shape, but it rarely refills them all the way, so a hard adventure keeps wearing on the party over time. That partialness is the point: one breather never erases the danger, and the heroes' choices accumulate across the whole Quest rather than resetting each time they rest. (More on why in the design note below.)

### The Tempo Cost

A Recovery Scene costs **time and momentum, not Readiness.** While the heroes catch their breath, the world doesn't pause: a threat creeps nearer, a trail goes cold, a deadline looms closer. The Guide (or, in Co-op, the group) narrates that pressure advancing.

Two things it specifically does **not** cost:

- It does **not** make you roll, and it does **not** risk any Readiness.
- It does **not** advance the **Antagonist Track.** (An earlier version of the game had resting mark a villain box; that rule is gone. The Antagonist Track moves *only* when the heroes flee a Challenge — Chapter 10.) The cost of resting is purely story tempo and world-pressure.

So a Recovery Scene is a real decision: heal now and let the world keep moving, or push on while your Readiness still holds? Because it's gated by safe fiction *and* costs tempo, it stays naturally infrequent — many short adventures need none at all.

### Out of Action

If a hero's Readiness hits **0**, they're **Out of Action** — too hurt, too rattled, or too tapped-out to keep going for now. They can't take actions until they recover. Remember the core promise: **heroes can't die.** Out of Action is the floor, and it's always temporary.

A downed hero comes back in one of two ways:

- **At the next Recovery Scene**, returning at **+3** along with everyone else.
- **Pulled back mid-Scene by an ally.** A teammate can spend their turn on **Aid Your Ally** (Chapter 8) to reach them — on a hit, the downed hero is back in the fight at **+3** and can act again. This is the in-the-moment rescue when there's no time for a proper breather.

Either way, nobody sits out for long — being knocked Out of Action is a dramatic low point to play, not a benching.

### The Victory Bump

Completing a Quest is the biggest breather of all. When the heroes fill the last box of the Quest Track, **each hero recovers +6 Readiness** — double a normal Recovery Scene — still **capped at 9.**

It's a *larger* Recovery Scene, not a full reset: even +6 won't push anyone past the cap, so a party that limped to victory still carries the wear of a hard Quest into whatever comes next. Because the bump triggers on **completing a Quest** (not on a tidy session boundary), it's also what lets **several Quests run at once** — finishing any one of them grants its bump, with no need for clean start/stop points. (Full multi-Quest guidance is in Chapter 10.)

### Design Note: Why Healing Is Never Full

Amazing Forge never lets you top all the way back up in one beat, and that's a deliberate choice. A full reset would erase the stakes — if every rest wiped the slate clean, Readiness loss would stop mattering and the smart play would be to grind rests for free safety. Partial healing (+3, or +6 on a win, always capped at 9) means **choices accumulate:** the bold risk you took two Scenes ago still shows on your track, and a long adventure genuinely wears the party down. It nudges the table to *push forward through the story* rather than stop and farm recovery — which keeps the movie moving and keeps the tension honest right up to the climax.
