# Chapter 10 — Story Arcs & the Antagonist Track

A **Story Arc** is the engine that gives an adventure shape: a goal worth chasing, the steps to get there, and a villain racing you for it. This chapter is the complete guide to setting one up and running it — the **Story Arc Track**, the **Antagonist Track** that runs alongside it, how to build good Milestones, what happens when you win, lose, or quit, and how to keep several Story Arcs going at once.

Everything here builds on tools from earlier chapters: Milestones are made of **Scenes** (Chapter 8), the heroes win or lose by filling tracks, and the Antagonist Track moves chiefly when the heroes **flee** (Chapter 8).

---

## Part A — Building & Winning a Story Arc

### Story Arc Structure

A Story Arc has three parts:

- **The Goal** — the single, clear victory condition, stated in a sentence ("Recover the stolen Sun Crystal," "Escape the sinking sky-city"). It's the finish line everyone can name.
- **The Milestones** — the major steps that get the heroes there. Each one is a real, pointable achievement, not a vague "keep going."
- **The two Tracks** — a **Story Arc Track** with one box per Milestone, and an **Antagonist Track** of equal length drawn right beneath it. The heroes win by filling the top row first; they lose if the bottom row fills first.

```
STORY ARC TRACK:    [ ]  [ ]  [ ]
ANTAGONIST TRACK:   [ ]  [ ]  [ ]
```

You don't plan *how* each Milestone gets done — that's what play discovers. You only name the Goal, the Milestones, and the antagonist, draw the two rows, and start in the middle of the action.

### Story Arc Types: Episode, Movie, Season, Series

Not every Story Arc is the same *size.* One can be a single afternoon's adventure or the spine of your whole game — and the best stories run several at once. Think of your favorite series. In **Avatar: The Last Airbender**, every episode opens and closes its own little story; every Book (Water, Earth, Fire) carries a season-long goal that resolves across many episodes; and all three Books together tell one long story — mastering the elements and stopping the Fire Lord. *Lights, Camera, Action!* builds your game the same way, with four **types** of Story Arc. They all use the **exact same machinery** — a Story Arc Track with a matching Antagonist Track beneath it — and the **exact same rule** for filling boxes (next section). The type is just a label for **how many boxes** the plotline gets and **how long it stays in your story:**

| Type | Boxes | It's done when… | Feels like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Episode** | **3** | you pack up tonight | one TV episode |
| **Movie** | **8** *(12 for an epic)* | you've told this one story over ~2–3 sessions | a feature film |
| **Season** | **8** | a thread you keep returning to across many sessions resolves | a show's season arc |
| **Series** | **12** | the whole game's backbone pays off | the whole show / the big bad |

Pick the type by asking one question: **"How long will this plotline matter?"** The four sort into two natural pairs:

- **Told in one push** — an **Episode** (short; your first games use only this) or a **Movie** (a bigger self-contained story you tell over a couple of sessions). You sit down to tell *this* story start to finish.
- **A throughline across other play** — a **Season** (a thread that runs through a stretch of episodes) or a **Series** (the handful of huge beats spanning the whole game). You return to it again and again while other arcs come and go.

**A box is one Milestone, not a fixed slice of time.** Played back-to-back — a Movie — a box is about 40 minutes, so eight of them fill across two or three sessions. As a Season throughline, with whole Episodes happening *between* its beats, those same eight boxes might span a dozen sessions. The box count sets the **story weight;** how much you play between the beats sets the **clock.** (Put another way: a "Season" with nothing played between its beats is just a Movie.) Box counts are defaults, not locks — stretch a meaty Episode to 4–5, or run an epic Movie at 12. **B-plots and character arcs are Story Arcs too** — usually Season- or Series-sized, run alongside your main arc.

**Reaching for a Movie, in practice.** Suppose the table wants one big self-contained story — *the heroes are trapped on a space station spiraling into its sun, with until dawn to get everyone off alive.* That's too much for a single sitting, but it's still **one story, start to finish**: no episodic side-trips, nothing larger sitting above it. Give it **8 boxes** and play it out over your next two or three sessions — that's a Movie. (Run that *same* premise as a place you keep returning to between other adventures and it'd be a Season instead — same eight boxes; the only difference is whether you *binge* it or *serialize* it.)

Choose an Episode's length at the start based on how much time you have, and **adjust mid-stream:** if real-world time is short, collapse a planned Milestone into a quick Scene or two and head for the finish; if the table's having a great time, let a Milestone breathe into several Scenes. The track shows exactly how much road is left, which makes pacing easy to eyeball (Chapter 12).

### The Headway Rule: One Milestone Can Serve Several Arcs

Here's the rule that fills every box, at every type: **mark a box whenever the table agrees you've made significant headway on that arc — a Milestone.** For an Episode, that's usually finishing a concrete step ("find the secret entrance"). For a Season or Series, it's any turning point that clearly moves the big goal — a hard-won conversation, a major discovery, a villain's scheme undone. If it feels like genuine progress on *that* arc, mark it.

The important part: **a single moment of play can be a Milestone for more than one arc at once.** When tonight's Episode climax *also* lands a blow on the season's villain, you mark a box on **both** tracks — not because the Episode "feeds" the Season, but because real headway genuinely happened on each. Ask any big beat three independent questions: *did this move the Episode? the Season? the Series?* Mark a box on every arc it advanced.

Back to Avatar:

- The heroes play out an Episode — *"Steal the waterbending scroll back from the pirates."* They win it: check off that 3-box Episode.
- That same climax was a real step toward the Season goal (*"Reach the North Pole and master water"*), so it also marks **one box** on the 8-box Season.
- Episodes later, the Season fills — they reach the North Pole and find their master. **Book One is won** — a beat big enough to mark **one box** on the 12-box **Series** too.

Two things follow naturally. **"Filler" is fine:** an Episode that moves no larger arc marks nothing above it, and that's true to the genre. And **long arcs have no guaranteed pace** — a Season advances only when the story actually moves it, so a run of filler can leave it parked. That's correct, not a problem: those filler Episodes are still paying off (each is its own Story Arc earning Growth, Chapter 13); the Season simply waits for a beat that genuinely matters.

### What Makes a Good Milestone (and Quick Ideas)

A Milestone is a **chapter of your movie** — and like a chapter, it's made of several **Scenes** (Chapter 8). A Scene is one beat of play: a quick **Regular Roll** or two, or a full **Challenge**. A Milestone is usually a short string of Scenes building to a payoff — **not** a single Challenge. (Occasionally a small Milestone resolves in one Scene, but the typical one is a little sequence.)

A good Milestone is **concrete and checkable.** "Find the secret entrance" is good — you'll know the moment it's done. "Make progress" is not. Aim for steps a viewer could point at and say "that's the part where they got into the mountain."

**Quick milestone ideas by Story Arc type.** Stuck for what the steps should be? Pick or riff off these shapes:

- **Rescue:** find the captive's location · get past what's guarding them · break them out · escape with them.
- **Heist:** case the target · get inside · reach the prize · get out clean (or not).
- **Journey:** survive the first leg · cross the major barrier · reach the threshold · arrive changed.
- **Investigation:** find the first clue · follow it to a witness or scene · uncover the hidden truth · confront who's behind it.
- **Confrontation:** gather what you need · weaken the foe's position · force the meeting · the final reckoning.

Mix and match — a Story Arc can blend shapes (a journey that ends in a heist, an investigation that turns into a rescue).

### Start a Story Arc: The Opening Roll

You've named the Goal, the Milestones, and the antagonist, and drawn the two tracks. One thing's left before the first scene: find out *how* the Story Arc opens. **Start a Story Arc** is the move that does it — a single roll, made once as the Story Arc begins, that hands you the tone of your launch and points you straight at your opening scene.

Roll **2d6 + 1.** No Asset applies here — this isn't a test of any hero's skill; it's the world deciding how things stand as you set out, so you get the same **+1** floor as any off-Asset action. (And **doubles still trigger Oracle's Blessing**, bumping your opening up a tier.)

Here's the important part: **this is a roll you can't fail.** All three results are good openings — they only differ in how much trouble is already on the heroes when the camera starts rolling. Read your result and let it shape that first scene:

| Roll | Your Story Arc opens… | How to play it |
|---|---|---|
| **10+ — Clear purpose** | You set out emboldened, the way ahead in plain sight. | Open on the **front foot.** The heroes are ready and in control as the scene begins, and *they* make the first move. Paint a confident launch and ask "What do you do?" |
| **7–9 — More questions than answers** | You're committed, but something's already unsettled. | Begin in motion with **one complication** already in play. Don't know what? **Ask the Oracle** (Chapter 11) or just decide — a missing supply, an unexpected face, a route that's changed. The first scene is about getting your footing. |
| **6− — Trouble finds you first** | A setback meets you before you've taken a step. | Open **in the thick of it,** on the back foot — the first scene is very likely a **Challenge** the heroes didn't choose. **Ask the Oracle** for the obstacle if it isn't obvious. This is the most cinematic launch of the three: you're already running. |

**Why a Miss is still a good thing.** Because nothing here is a hero attempting a risky action, a "Miss" isn't a failure — it's just the most dramatic way *in.* Over a long game, most Story Arcs will open with *some* immediate tension and a few will open calm, and that mix is exactly the pacing a good series has. (A show where every episode started in a quiet tavern would be a dull one.)

**Turning the result into a scene.** The roll gives you a *temperature,* not a script — you still supply the content, and the content always points at your **first Milestone.** Say your first Milestone is "find the secret entrance":

- A **10+** opens with the heroes standing at the cliff face, rested and ready, scanning the rock for the hidden door.
- A **7–9** opens the same way, but the map was wrong — the door isn't where it should be, and the light's already fading.
- A **6−** opens with them clinging to that cliff in a howling storm, a goblin patrol closing in below.

Same Milestone, three different heats. Roll, read the temperature, paint the picture, and ask "What do you do?"

> **First games skip this roll.** Part One (Chapter 3) just has you open in the middle of the action — no roll needed. The opening roll is a Part Two tool for when you want the dice to surprise you with how a new Story Arc begins.

### Finishing a Story Arc (and the Optional Story Arc Showdown)

Fill the last box of the Story Arc Track and the **Story Arc is won.** Play out the victory — finishing **prompts Downtime** (the long rest), so each hero recovers **+6 Readiness**, capped at 9 (Chapter 9).

For a climax that deserves a final-act turn, you can make that last box a **Story Arc Showdown** (the Story Arc-level version of the Showdown from Chapter 8): completing the closing Milestone takes one last roll.

- **Strong Hit:** the Story Arc is won, gloriously — add a narrative bonus.
- **Weak Hit:** won, but Pay the Price on the way out.
- **Miss:** *you thought it was over, but it wasn't.* Add **one extra Milestone box** to the Story Arc Track and drop a climactic twist — the villain's true plan surfaces, an ally turns, the prize is a fake. Complete that new Milestone, then you can attempt the Story Arc Showdown again.

A missed Story Arc Showdown **never** adds an Antagonist box. The twist simply gives the heroes more story to play, not the villains a free win. It's optional; use it when an ending deserves a surprise, and let ordinary Story Arcs close cleanly when the last box fills.

## Part B — The Antagonist Track (Core Rule)

### What It Is

Every Story Arc is a **race between two tracks.** The Story Arc Track is the heroes' progress; the **Antagonist Track** is the antagonist's progress toward *their* goal, running in parallel. It is **the losing side of the same arc** — not a separate Story Arc, but this arc's other end — and a **core part of every Story Arc,** never optional. You always draw it, because it's the backbone of the heroes' stay-alive valve (fleeing) and the only way a Story Arc can be lost.

Each of its boxes is one of the antagonist's *own* milestones — the dragon spreading its fire, the rival crew closing on the same treasure, the storm marching toward the city.

### Drawing the Track

The Antagonist Track has the **same number of boxes as the Story Arc Track** (Episode 3 / Movie 8 / Season 8 / Series 12), drawn as a matching row right beneath it. Equal length means it's a true race — the villains need exactly as many wins as the heroes do, at every scale.

You don't have to spell out all of the villain's milestones in advance. It's enough to know who they are and what they want; you'll decide what each filled box *means* when it happens (see the Surge).

### Characterize Your Antagonist

Before you can mark a box for "the bad guys gaining ground," you need to know **who the bad guys are** — and the antagonist isn't always a *who*. Sometimes the thing standing between the heroes and their goal is a **force**: a harsh desert, a closing-in blizzard, a spreading plague, the simple fact that *time is running out*. That's a perfectly good antagonist — but a faceless one is hard to picture, and you'll want a clear image when a box fills.

So **give your antagonist a face.** Decide what it *wants* and what "winning" looks like for it, even if it's not a person:

- Crossing a brutal desert? The **desert** is your antagonist — it wants to wear you down and swallow you. A filled box is a sandstorm, a dry well, a collapsing dune.
- Racing a sickness through the village? The **plague** is the antagonist; each box is another street it reaches.
- Up against a scheming duke? Easy — *he's* the antagonist, and each box is a move in his plan.

Naming this turns every Antagonist tick into a concrete story beat instead of an abstract counter. You're not "marking box two of four" — *the sandstorm hits.*

> *That's all the Antagonist Track needs: a face and a goal. To make a *person* antagonist into a memorable **character** — a villain with a personality worth squaring off against — see [Chapter 16](../part-three/16-cool-characters.md), which builds villains from the same archetypes as heroes.*

### How It Fills

The Antagonist Track advances when **the antagonist gains ground** — and the table decides, in the fiction, when that's happened. There's one rock-solid trigger plus a couple of judgment calls:

- **Fleeing a Challenge (the core trigger).** When the group decides a Challenge is costing more Readiness than it's worth, they **flee** (Chapter 8): abandon it, lose its progress, and mark **one box** on the Antagonist Track. The villains gained ground because the heroes gave ground. This is the everyday, reliable way the track moves — especially for Episodes.
- **A telling failure, at the table's discretion.** Sometimes a **significant** Miss or a costly Weak Hit clearly hands the antagonist an opening, even though the heroes didn't flee. When that happens and the table agrees it fits, you **may** mark an Antagonist box. This is the main way **Season and Series** villains advance — you rarely "flee" a season-long arc, but its villain absolutely creeps forward when things go badly. (This is folded into **Pay the Price**, Chapter 7: when you Pay the Price, you *may* also tick an Antagonist box — and if you do, narrate what the antagonist just gained, exactly as you would after a flee.)

The key principle: ticking the Antagonist Track is always a **deliberate call**, never an automatic punishment for one bad roll. The track doesn't lurch forward every time the dice are unkind — it moves when the *story* says the bad guys got ahead. That keeps losing a Story Arc from being something bad luck *does to* the players; it's the cumulative result of moments the table looked at and agreed, "yeah — they're winning this one."

What does **not**, on its own, advance it: an ordinary Weak Hit or Miss (those just cost Readiness), recovery (Mend or Downtime), or a missed Showdown.

### The Surge: Playing Out a Villain Milestone

When an Antagonist box fills, **stop and play it out** — this beat is called the **Surge.** The villains just completed a milestone of their own, so:

1. **Envision what that looks like off-screen for them.** What did the antagonist accomplish? The cult finished the first ritual; the warlord seized the bridge; the flood reached the lower town; the **sandstorm** rolled in and buried the trail.
2. **Bring it on-screen as a complication.** A new obstacle appears, a threat escalates, an ally is captured, the deadline jumps closer. The world should visibly tighten around the heroes.

A Surge is an open invitation to bend the plot. It's the payoff that makes giving ground *feel* costly even when it saved the party's Readiness — the story just got harder in a concrete, dramatic way. (And because you **characterized your antagonist** earlier, you always know whose face to put on it — even when the "villain" is a desert.)

## Part C — Losing, Quitting & Juggling Story Arcs

### Losing & Quitting a Story Arc Gracefully

If the Antagonist Track fills before the Story Arc Track, the bad guys win and the **Story Arc is lost.** The heroes can also choose to **Quit the Story Arc** outright at any time — the terminal version of fleeing, when the group decides this one simply isn't winnable or worth the cost.

Losing carries **no mechanical penalty and no bonus.** There's no extra Readiness loss for quitting and no Downtime heal for it; the heroes just step away, battered but alive, and the world moves on without their win.

And crucially, **a lost Story Arc is not a dead end — it's a seed.** When the antagonists win, ask the table together:

> *"How did the world change now that they got what they wanted?"*

Then let that answer reshape your setting and spark the **next** Story Arc — directly or indirectly born from this defeat. The cult completed its ritual: now the sky is wrong and a new Story Arc is to undo it. The rival crew got the treasure: now they're powerful, and the heroes want it back. A good loss should leave the table *excited* about what happens next, not deflated. Because heroes can't die, "losing" is just the story taking a turn — exactly the kind of turn a great series thrives on.

### Running Multiple Story Arcs at Once

You're not limited to one Story Arc at a time. Several can be active simultaneously, each with its **own paired Story Arc and Antagonist Tracks** — and this is exactly how the four types live together. At any given moment a long game might have **one Series, one or two Seasons, and whatever Episode is in front of the heroes tonight**, all on the sheet at once. You play the Episode scene-to-scene; the bigger tracks tick forward whenever a beat genuinely moves them.

> **Setting up a long game.** Don't draw all four types on day one. Start with the **Series** — a sentence or two naming where the whole story is ultimately headed (*"Master the elements and defeat the Fire Lord"*), plus its 12-box pair. Add a **Season** once a medium-term thread sharpens into view (*"Master earthbending"*). Then just **play Episodes** session to session — and whenever an Episode's climax truly moves the Season or the Series, mark a box up there too. The big tracks fill themselves as a by-product of playing the small ones; you almost never sit down to "play the Series" directly.
>
> **B-plots and character arcs work the same way** — each as its own track off to the side. A hero's personal arc — *"Kaya learns to forgive the brother who betrayed her"* — is a Season-length Story Arc with its own Antagonist Track (here, her own pride and grief). It advances only at the beats that genuinely move it, usually *inside* an Episode about something else entirely; when one lands, mark a box. It resolves on its own schedule — no faster than the story earns it.

**Give each arc a role.** Alongside its Type, it helps to note what *job* an arc does in the story — purely a guide to how often you return to it, never a rule:

- **Main plot** — the spotlight driver, returned to most sessions (usually your Series or current Season).
- **Side plot** — a secondary thread you touch periodically, letting it heat up or cool (writers call these the A- and B-plots).
- **Character arc** — a side plot that belongs to one hero (like Kaya's, above); surface it in *their* spotlight beats, and it pays off when they change.

A role isn't fixed — a side plot that catches fire just *becomes* the main plot — and it never touches the dice. **Type** sets the boxes; the **role** only tells you how to *play* the arc.

A whole **Avatar**-style game laid out:

```
SERIES    — Master the elements, defeat the Fire Lord
STORY ARC:    [✓][✓][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ]   (Books Water & Earth done)
ANTAGONIST:   [✓][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ]   (the comet draws closer)

SEASON    — Master Earthbending (Book Two)
STORY ARC:    [✓][✓][✓][✓][ ][ ][ ][ ]
ANTAGONIST:   [✓][✓][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ][ ]   (the Dai Li tighten their grip)

EPISODE   — Escape the Library before it sinks
STORY ARC:    [✓][ ][ ]
ANTAGONIST:   [ ][ ][ ]
```

Win tonight's Episode and — if that win was a real step toward the Season — you mark a box on the Season Track too; fill the Season Track with a big enough beat and you mark the Series. That's the whole system breathing. It falls naturally out of two rules already in play: finishing a Story Arc prompts Downtime — triggered on *completing an arc,* not on a session boundary — and losing a Story Arc *seeds* another — so Story Arcs are always starting and finishing at different times rather than the table marching through one at a time.

To keep it manageable:

- **Track each Story Arc as its own pair of rows** on the sheet, clearly labeled with its scale. Don't merge them — each race is separate.
- **Mark the big tracks when big beats happen.** A Season or Series moves whenever a Scene genuinely advances it — most often the climax of tonight's Episode, but any real turning point counts (the Headway Rule).
- **Let Story Arcs cross-pollinate.** Completing one can change the situation in another (freeing an ally who then helps; angering a faction that now opposes you). That's a feature — it's how a setting starts to feel alive.
- **Keep one Story Arc in focus at a time at the table.** Multiple Story Arcs can *exist*, but a single Scene is usually about one of them — almost always the Episode. Switch focus at natural breaks so parallel stories don't fragment the group's attention.

For most groups — and almost all first games — a single Episode is plenty. Add a Season Track once the table wants threads that carry across adventures, and a Series Track once you're committing to the long haul.

> **Story Arc Seeds (sidebar).** Stuck for a premise? Grab one of these and bend it to your Worldbuilding. Each is just a spark — a Goal and a hint of trouble, not a plotted adventure.
>
> - A beloved local landmark has begun to *vanish* a little more each night — find out why before it's gone for good.
> - Someone is impersonating one of the heroes, and the fake got there first.
> - The only bridge to the festival washed out this morning; the festival is tonight.
> - A sealed door that's been shut for a hundred years just creaked open from the *inside*.
> - The harvest (or the power, or the water) will run out in three days unless its source is found.
> - A messenger collapses at your feet clutching a map you're not supposed to be able to read.
> - The town's protector — a guardian, a machine, a spirit — has stopped working, and no one will say why.
> - A rival you thought you'd beaten is back, and this time they brought friends.
