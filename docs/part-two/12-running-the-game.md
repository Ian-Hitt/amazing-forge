# Chapter 12 — Running the Game (Complete)

Everything so far has been about *what* happens when the dice hit the table. This chapter is about *running* the table itself — the human craft of keeping a story moving, whether one person is steering as the **Guide** or the whole group is steering together in **Co-op**.

The two modes share more than they differ. Both live on the same handful of skills: asking the table good questions, voicing a world that has no stat blocks, and reading the clock so the story lands instead of fizzling. So this chapter is built in three parts: running with a Guide, running Co-op, and the shared tools that serve both. Part One introduced these briefly across Chapters 5 and 6 — this is the full version.

> **You already have less to do than you think.** The hardest part of most games — inventing the world and plotting the adventure — is already handled. The **World Forge** (Chapter 14) built your setting. The **Quest Track** (Chapter 10) points at where the story is going. The **Oracle** (Chapter 11) answers what happens next when you're stuck. Running the game isn't about carrying all of that in your head; it's about responding to what's in front of you, one scene at a time.

---

## Part A — Playing with a Guide

In **Guided play**, one person steps into the role of the **Guide** — part director, part oracle, part narrator of everything that isn't a player's hero. It's a big-sounding job that's smaller than it looks, because the Guide's task is never to *plan* the story. It's to *respond* to it.

### The Guide's Job

You have four real jobs at the table. None of them is "decide what happens."

- **Frame the scenes.** Cut the movie. Open each scene in the middle of something interesting (*in media res*), describe what the heroes see, and ask **"What do you do?"** When a scene has given everything it's going to give, cut to the next one — don't let it idle.
- **Voice the world.** You play everyone who isn't a hero: the nervous merchant, the circling dragon, the storm rolling in off the sea. (How to do that without stat blocks is in [Part C](#voicing-npcs).)
- **Advance the pressure.** You hold the **Antagonist** (Chapter 10). When the heroes rest, stall, or flee, the world keeps moving — and you're the one who shows them how.
- **Ask great questions.** Your most powerful tool isn't an answer; it's a question thrown back to the table. More on that in [Part C](#asking-the-table-good-questions).

What's missing from that list is the part new Guides expect to carry: the plot. You are not writing an adventure the heroes walk through. You're setting scenes in motion and finding out — alongside everyone else — how they go.

### No Prep: How to Improvise Well

"Zero prep" is a promise, not a dare. You are genuinely not expected to prepare anything. But improvising well is a skill, and these habits make it feel effortless:

- **Lean on what's already on the table.** Every World Forge answer is loaded ammunition. The **Threats**, the **Forbidden**, the **Leadership**, the **Reputation** — you wrote those down precisely so you'd never face a blank page. Stuck for a complication? Reach for a Threat. Stuck for a place? You already named the world's geography.
- **Read the next Milestone off the track.** You always know roughly where the story is heading, because the Quest Track tells you: the next empty box is the next chapter. You don't need to know *how* the heroes get there — just point every scene loosely toward it.
- **Make the next scene inevitable *and* surprising.** The trick to satisfying improvisation is that the next beat should feel like it had to happen — *of course* the bridge guard recognizes them — while still landing as a surprise. You get there by building on what's established (inevitable) and adding one thing nobody expected (surprise).
- **Say yes, or yes-but.** When a player tries something clever, your default is yes. If it shouldn't be free, it's a **Challenge** or a roll — "yes, *but* you'll have to get past the guards first." Flat no's stall the movie; turn them into obstacles instead.
- **When you don't know, ask the table.** This is the release valve that makes no-prep possible. You are never obligated to have an answer ready — see [Asking the Table Good Questions](#asking-the-table-good-questions).

### Managing Readiness Pressure

Here's the counterintuitive heart of being a Guide: **your job is not to knock the heroes out.** Heroes can't die (Chapter 8), and grinding their **Readiness** to zero isn't a win for you — it's a sign the pressure stopped feeling like a story and started feeling like arithmetic.

Your real job is to make Readiness feel **precious.** A number that drains predictably is boring; a number that drains *dramatically* is tension. So:

- **Make every Price a scene, not a fee.** When a hero takes a Weak Hit or a Miss, **Pay the Price** (Chapter 9) is your moment to push the story, not just subtract a point. The floor doesn't crumble because a rule says "−2" — it crumbles because that's the most exciting thing that could happen right now, and *then* it costs Readiness.
- **Let low Readiness be a cue, not a threat.** When the party's running thin, that's not your signal to pounce — it's the table's signal that a **Recovery Scene** (Chapter 9) is due, or that it might be time to **Flee** (Chapter 8). A good Guide reads that moment out loud: "You're both pretty battered — is this a fight you want to finish?"
- **Save the squeeze for moments that matter.** If you treat every roll as a chance to hurt someone, the hurt stops meaning anything. Spend your hardest complications on the beats that deserve weight — the climax of a Challenge, a Surge, the threshold of the villain's lair.

Pressure done right makes the heroes *feel* the danger without ever taking them off the board. That feeling — "we might not make it" — is the whole point.

## Part B — Playing Co-op (No Guide)

**Co-op play** runs the same game with the Guide's chair empty. Nobody is the director, because *everybody* is — the directing duties get shared around the table, and the **Oracle** becomes the engine that answers the questions a Guide would otherwise field.

### How Co-op Differs

The thing to understand up front: **Co-op is not freeform make-believe.** It can feel like it should be looser without a Guide, but it's actually *more* structured, not less. With no single person holding the story together, the mechanics do that job — the tracks, the Oracle, the turn loop. You lean on the system harder, and it pays you back by generating a story none of you planned.

Concretely, three things move from "the Guide does it" to "the table does it together":

- **Framing scenes** — shared, popcorn-style (below).
- **Answering unknowns** — handed to the **Oracle** (Chapter 11) instead of a Guide's gut.
- **Voicing the world** — split around the table ([Part C](#voicing-npcs)).

Everything else — the Roll, Challenges, Readiness, Quests — works exactly as written.

### The Golden Rule

When in doubt, **do the obvious or coolest next thing.**

That's the whole philosophy of Co-op in one line. When the table isn't sure what happens next, you don't need permission and you don't need a vote — you reach for whichever of those two is sitting right there:

- The **obvious** thing is whatever the story has been setting up. If the heroes just heard the dam is about to burst, the obvious next beat is the race to stop it.
- The **coolest** thing is the one that makes everyone lean in. When two ideas tie, pick the one that sounds more like a great movie.

**Breaking ties.** If the table genuinely splits and nobody's budging, don't grind on it — hand it to the **Oracle** (Chapter 11). Ask the Dice settles a yes/no; the Story Spark breaks an open "which way?" Let the dice decide and *commit* — a committed choice beats a perfect one that never gets made.

### Scene Transitions (Popcorn)

In Guided play the Guide cuts every scene. In Co-op, that power rotates — **popcorn-style,** the same way turns rotate inside a Challenge (Chapter 8).

The rule of thumb: **whoever narrated the last cut hands the next one to someone else.** You set up a scene, frame the situation, and ask "what do you do?" — and when that scene wraps, you pass the camera. This keeps any one player from quietly becoming the de-facto Guide and keeps the spotlight moving around the table.

It doesn't have to be rigid. The point isn't strict turn-tracking; it's the *habit* of passing the camera so the story stays everyone's.

### Who Decides Pay the Price

When a roll comes up a Weak Hit or a Miss, **the player who rolled has final say over the complication that hits their own hero.**

This is deliberate. You know your hero best, and getting to author your own setback — "my grappling hook snaps and I lose the high ground" — is more fun than having one done *to* you. It also keeps Co-op from turning into players gently sandbagging each other.

If the rolling player draws a blank, **brainstorm as a table** — toss out options, grab the most cinematic one (the **d10 Pay the Price** table in Chapter 9 is there for exactly this), and the rolling player makes the final call. Then **commit and move on.** Don't litigate it.

### Staying Unstuck (Ask the Oracle as Your Engine)

In Guided play, the Oracle is a Guide's helper. In Co-op, **the Oracle is the engine** — it's not the thing you reach for when all else fails, it's the thing you reach for *first* whenever the table doesn't already know what happens next.

Use it **proactively, not reactively.** The difference matters:

- *Reactive (don't):* sit in silence, feel stuck, eventually remember the Oracle exists, roll out of desperation.
- *Proactive (do):* the moment a scene wraps and the next one isn't obvious, name the next **Milestone**, then **do the obvious** or **roll the Story Spark** to point yourselves at it. You're not rescuing a stalled game; you're driving it.

The full ladder — name the Milestone, do the obvious, Story Spark, Ask the Dice, and how to chain results without spiraling — is Chapter 11. In Co-op you'll use it constantly, and that's exactly right.

### Deciding to Flee Without a Guide

Fleeing a Challenge (Chapter 8) is always a **group decision**, and in Co-op there's no Guide to frame the moment — so the table has to notice it together.

The question to ask out loud is simple: **"Is the Readiness bleed still worth it — or do we cut our losses and let the villain gain ground?"** That framing matters, because **Fleeing is a tactic, not a failure.** You trade *story ground* (the Challenge's lost progress, plus one box on the **Antagonist Track**) for *survival* (the bleeding stops). A party that never flees isn't braver — it's just one bad Challenge away from going **Out of Action** for no reason.

Make it a shared call, and make it a *story* beat, not a punishment: narrate the pull-back, play out the **Surge** (Chapter 10) as the antagonist gains ground, and keep going. And remember the floor — because the Antagonist Track is short, you can only flee so many times before it fills and you **Quit the Quest** (Chapter 10). That's what makes each retreat a real decision instead of a free reset.

## Part C — Shared Tools (Both Modes)

These three skills are the actual craft of running a game, and they serve a Guide and a Co-op table identically. Whoever's holding the camera in a given scene is using them.

### Asking the Table Good Questions

The single most useful move in either mode is to **turn a question back to the table.** *"What scary thing is blocking the bridge?"* *"Why doesn't this town trust strangers?"* The answers a group invents together are richer than anything one person produces alone — and the player who names the scary thing is instantly invested in it.

This is what makes no-prep work. You never have to know everything, because the table is a generator you can tap any time. The skill is in *how* you ask:

- **Ask open, not yes/no.** "Is there a guard?" gets you a shrug. "What kind of guard is posted here, and why does it matter to you?" gets you a character. (If you genuinely want a yes/no answer the fiction hasn't settled, that's a job for **Ask the Dice**, Chapter 11 — not the table.)
- **Aim the question at someone's hero.** "Your Asset is *Grew Up on These Streets* — so what's the one shortcut everybody else has forgotten?" You get an answer *and* you spotlight a player.
- **Build on the answer, don't overwrite it.** When someone offers an idea, the next words out of your mouth should use it, not correct it. Overwriting a player's contribution once teaches the table to stop contributing.
- **Don't ask away the tension.** Ask questions to *build* the world and *raise* stakes — not to hand the heroes an easy out. "How do you happen to have exactly the key you need?" is a question that deflates the scene.

### Voicing NPCs

Remember that **NPCs have no stats and no Readiness** (Chapter 8). A guard captain, a talking fox, a dying star — they're all purely *fictional* forces. There's no block to read; there's only the question of what they want and how they push on the scene.

That's freeing. To make an NPC feel real, you only need three things: **a want** (what are they after?), **a single defining trait** (gruff? oily? terrified?), and **a way they push on the heroes.** Pick those on the spot and the character comes alive — no preparation required.

In any given moment, an NPC is doing one of four things. Knowing which keeps them sharp:

- **Pushing back** — an obstacle. (This is usually a **Challenge**, Chapter 8 — the NPC *is* the track.)
- **Helping** — giving information, a tool, a way through.
- **Complicating** — wanting something that cuts across what the heroes want.
- **Getting out of the way** — when a scene is over, let the NPC exit. Don't keep them on stage past their purpose.

> **The Co-op NPC rule.** When you speak for an NPC, **your own hero steps back** from that exchange — you can't really argue with yourself, so let the other players deal with the NPC, especially for back-and-forth dialogue. For a big, important NPC the table can **group-narrate** instead, deciding together what they say and do. Group-narration is great for major moments but it's slow, so save it for the ones that earn it.

### Pacing: The Story Clock

Pacing is what separates an adventure that *lands* from one that runs out of time mid-scene. You have two dials, and the art is in reading the room and turning them.

**Dial 1 — Track difficulty.** Every track you draw is a decision about screen time. A **Hard Challenge** (more boxes, Chapter 8) eats a bigger chunk of the session than an Easy one. If real-world time is running short, draw *easier* tracks to push the story toward its conclusion; if the table is savoring things and has time to spare, draw bigger.

**Dial 2 — Milestone timing (the 40-Minute Rule).** The **Milestone** is your most powerful pacing tool, because nothing strictly defines how long one takes — and the **Quest Track** shows you exactly how many remain, so you can always gauge where you are.

| The plan | Quest scale | Pace | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The 2-hour adventure** | Episodic (3 boxes) | ~**40 minutes** per Milestone | ~2 hours |
| **The 4-hour adventure** | Episodic (3 boxes) | ~**80 minutes** per Milestone | ~4 hours |

These aren't timers to obey — they're a yardstick. Glance at the clock and the empty boxes together: if you're an hour in with all three boxes still open, it's time to resolve Challenges faster. If you've got time and energy to spare, slow down and add scene detail.

**Reading the room.** The clock is only half of it; the table is the other half. Slow down when a scene is generating real drama or character feeling — that's the good stuff, let it breathe. Speed up when energy dips, when a scene has given everything it has, or when you catch the table waiting for the *next* thing. Trust the lean-in: when everyone's leaning forward, stay; when they're glancing away, cut.

**Skip or play it out?** Not every beat deserves a full scene. The question is the same one you ask to draw a Challenge (Chapter 8): *how much of the movie should this be?*

- **Montage it** when the outcome isn't in doubt and the journey isn't the point. "Three days of hard travel later, you crest the ridge and see the city." One sentence; move on.
- **Play it out** when there's real tension, a meaningful choice, or a moment a player has been waiting for. That's what the screen time is *for.*

**Ending a session well.** Sessions end on the clock, but they shouldn't *feel* like they ended on the clock. As you near time, steer toward a natural stopping point — a **cliffhanger** ("the door swings open and standing there is —" *next time*) or a **breather** (a Recovery Scene, a quiet victory, a campfire). Either one is a deliberate landing. "Well, we're out of time, I guess we'll pick up wherever" is not a landing — and with a little steering you almost never have to settle for it.
